## The second source

The first week of this blog had a simple explanation: an old backlog draining.
Ten posts in seven days is not a writing speed, it is the emptying of a store that took thirty years to build.

An expectation followed: once the store is empty, the pace falls back to whatever new thoughts supply.

That expectation has a gap.
It counts on one source, and there are two.

The second one is the work itself.
Every session produces transcripts, every decision produces commits, every tool produces measurements, and each of those traces can be turned into a post.
Six of the fourteen published posts come from that source.
It does not run dry as long as work continues — on the contrary: the more the blog writes about its own machinery, the more machinery there is to write about.

A store empties.
A feedback loop resupplies.

## Why that is dangerous

The obvious objection to a blog that writes about itself is tedium.
That is not the serious objection.

The serious one is the missing authority against which a wrong claim breaks.

Four posts here are about a laptop.
In every single one of them a theory that looked good breaks: the silent speaker was not a driver fault but a number the sound server had remembered.
The volume was not set wrong but frozen by a repair made years ago.
The machine did not boot faster because a configuration took hold — the configuration loaded cleanly and did nothing.

Every time the reason was the same: the machine has an opinion, and it is not mine.
It answers to measurements, not to phrasing.

A text about your own blog has no such authority.
If I claim a rule changed the work, there is nobody to contradict me — except myself, and I am the author of the claim.
The tool does not check the statement about the tool.

Which is why outside material is not variety here.
It is error correction.

## What went live twice

This blog's history contains two corrections to factual claims that were already published.

The first time, a middle speaker had been turned wholesale into a third one — a true sentence made false, and it stood in public.
The second time, the claim was that a laptop stopped booting from a certain kernel version onwards.
In fact it booted; the screen stayed dark.
Two follow-on sentences rested on the same wrong premise and had to go with it.

Both errors concern the same kind of sentence: a statement about something past for which there is no log and no commit.
Measurements get checked.
What happened back then gets reconstructed — and then written down with the same confidence.

A human found both while proofreading.
No checking program can do that, because no checking program knows what happened back then.

That has since become a procedure: before publishing, every claim about a past event that has no log, no commit, no measurement and no transcript is pulled out and submitted for confirmation.
For the first part of this series that was three sentences.
All three were confirmed; one of them would have shifted the point of a whole section had it not held.

## Three expectations

So that the series does not end on a mood, here are three sentences with numbers, recomputable in four weeks.

**One: the pace falls below one post per day.**
Just under twenty posts including drafts in seven days is the starting level.
The backlog is finite, and the second source supplies more slowly because it is tied to actual work.

**Two: the ratio of maintenance to new work stays around two to one, or climbs.**
86 to 39 is the starting value.
If it climbs sharply, structure is eating the writing; that would be the point at which an ordering costs posts instead of opening them up.

**Three: the share of posts about the blog itself falls.**
Six out of fourteen is the starting value.
The store of tooling stories is smaller than it currently feels: there is a limited number of procedures, and each can be told well once.

Any of those three numbers can be wrong.
If one is, it will be said here, with the same effort as the prediction.

## What cannot be predicted

The wish to turn all of this into a model that computes the next four weeks is understandable and hopeless.

Systems of this kind show their behaviour only by running.
You cannot skip a few steps and look ahead at where it goes; there is no shortcut through the computation.
That holds for the logistic map with its single line as much as for a blog with four series, nine tools and a rules file.

What remains is unspectacular: let it run, write it down, recompute in four weeks.

## Lessons learned

- **A well-functioning feedback loop has a supply problem, not a drying-up problem.** It does not stop; it quietly replaces the source it feeds on with itself.
- **Outside material is the error correction, not the variety.** A device has an opinion; a text about your own work has none.
- **Checking programs only find what is formally wrong.** What is factually wrong is found by a human who knows how it was — and who reliably finds it only once reading the finished text.
- **Expectations with numbers are cheaper than expectations without.** They cost almost nothing and make being wrong demonstrable instead of letting it disappear.
- **Declining to predict is not a lack of ambition.** It follows from the subject: where there is no shortcut, letting it run is the only method.

## What carries over

If you work with an agent and write about that work, one piece of bookkeeping pays: count how many of your texts draw their material from outside that work.

If the share falls below a quarter, you no longer have a report, you have a circuit.
You will not notice it in the writing — writing is unusually easy inside a circuit, because the material is always already there.
You notice it when nothing surprises you any more.

The remedy is inelegant and it works: work on something that does not answer because it likes you.
A device, a measurement, somebody else's dataset.
Anything that can say no.
