The honest answer to whether a human writes this is: partly.
What makes it interesting is that the parts come apart fairly cleanly.

## TL;DR

- The content is mine without exception so far: every measurement, every decision, every opinion, every retraction.
- The shape is usually Claude's — and it is usually better than mine.
- The evidence is in the posts themselves.
  No model knows which speaker on my laptop stays silent.
- The seam between the two is easiest to see where language refuses to cooperate: *erfahren* and *erlaufen*.
- What persists between sessions is a memory made of text files.
  It holds no knowledge about code, only working agreements — each one from a moment when something surprised me: going wrong, or going better than expected.
- What carries over: keep the material, give away the shape.
  The other way round produces text nobody needed.

## What comes from me

The content, completely and without exception.

Every number in the [Reverberations](/series/reverberations/) series comes from one particular laptop on one particular evening.
That the knob board on my desk carries two microcontrollers, and that the orientation of the USB plug decides which of them appears, is not something a model knows by itself — it is something you know after losing half an hour to it.
The decision that half-finished notes about this blog may go to my phone but not into somebody else's VM is mine.
The judgement about which claim holds and which does not is mine.
So are the retractions: one of the earliest posts here contains a theory I withdrew in public, because a measurement had finished it off.

There is a part of this that looks less like authorship and is authorship all the same: the deleting.
A text does not become mine because I read it over.
It becomes mine when sentences disappear that I would not put my name to.

Part two of this series even contains a case of exactly that.
My raw notes said the logistic equation "still cannot be simulated" — jotted down so briefly that, taken literally, the sentence is wrong.
It surfaced during preparation — the machine flagged the sentence and put two more precise versions next to it.
I decided.
That is what the division of labour looks like in practice: the objection can come from anywhere, the responsibility cannot.

## What comes from Claude

The shape.
Usually, and usually better than mine.

The order of the sections.
The place where an example is needed, and the place where the second example is one too many.
The summary at the top, which I would never write myself, because while writing I already know what is in there.
The second version in English.
The transitions that turn five scraps of notes into a text.

That this works well is not surprising once you say it out loud: a model trained on an enormous amount of human-written text, which additionally learns within the session, has seen more cases of "in what order does a person understand this?" than I will in a lifetime.

The surprise is the same every time regardless.
I hand over material and get back a shape I had not planned.
Reading it over, I decide whether it holds.
It usually holds.

## Where the seam shows

A while ago I read a thought whose source I can no longer find, so this is a paraphrase: AI learns from us only for as long as it takes to know enough about the analogue world to *erfahren* it directly through sensors it builds itself, and to *erlaufen* it with actuators of its own design.

My assessment of that is sober: overstated, for now at least.
Sensors and actuators have existed for a long time, and even so the whole load in that sentence is carried by the word "enough".

The sentence is interesting for an entirely different reason.
It does not survive translation, and the two German words above are the reason it is still standing here in German.

*Erfahren* means both things at once: to experience or to find something out — and to cover a distance by driving.
*Erlaufen* means to reach something by walking to it.
Both words tie knowledge to movement, and both do it with the same prefix.
That is the point of the original: in German, sensing and acting hang from a single image.

English has no such pair.
The closest thing is *to grasp*, which really does mean both to take hold of something and to understand it — but it only covers the sensing half.
There is no matching verb for the other half, no way to say "reached it by walking" and "came to know it" in one breath.

So this passage in English is not a translation of the German one.
It is the compromise I chose: keep the German words, show what they do, and admit that the English version has a hole where a pun used to be.
Put the two versions side by side and this is where you see the seam.

And that is what I am actually after here.
Not that a machine could not build a pun — it can, and rather well.
Rather that deciding what happens to an untranslatable sentence — leave it, rebuild it, or expose it — is a decision about what the text is for.
That decision belongs to whoever owns the text.

## What reading it over looks like

The road from an idea to a post has a fixed shape by now:

1. The idea becomes a note in an Obsidian vault, with a status and a source.
2. The note becomes an interview — questions with proposed answers that I click rather than type, because typing is the expensive part for me.
3. Out of that comes the German and English pair, both as drafts.
4. I read them over, delete, reorder, disagree.
5. Three small checking scripts run: one compares the minified build against the plain one, one checks the topic taxonomy, one checks the vault.
6. Only then does it go online.

One step in that chain is easy to overlook and is the most important one.
Before a post is published, its note collects a list of every statement backed by neither a log nor a measurement — sentences about my own past, for instance.
Those I confirm one by one, or they go.
For this series the list is long, because it is the most personal thing that has stood here so far.

## What learns along the way

What persists beyond the single session is a memory: a `MEMORY.md` as an index, with small text files next to it, one fact per file.

It holds no knowledge about my code — that is what the code is for.
It holds working agreements.
That I type without umlauts and that typos should not be commented on.
That a context reset should be proposed to me rather than waited for.
That quotations get translated in the English version rather than left standing.

Every one of those lines comes from a moment when something surprised me — going wrong, or going better than expected.
Together they are what is left of a working day once the context has been cleared.

Which leads straight to the next question, out of the previous part.
A memory is a selection medium: whatever is in it gets reused, whether or not it is still true.
That is what I will write about next, in a series of its own.

## What carries over

Keep the material, give away the shape.

The reason is not moral, it is division of labour.
Nobody can take the material off your hands — the shape they can, and they do it well.
The other way round, with the machine supplying the substance while you polish the phrasing, you get text that looks like a post and has nobody who needed it.

There is a simple test for this.
Does your text contain at least one number, one date or one sound that nobody but you could have contributed?

If it does, the question of who wrote it has already been answered.
