Ten posts in seven days is the kind of number that makes people suspicious.
I would be too.

## The number

The first commit of this blog sits on 31 August 2026, and the count ends on 6 September.
That is seven days.

They hold ten published posts and one more still in draft.
Since every post appears in German and in English, that comes to twenty-two versions of text.
A little over one post per day on average.
On 5 September there were three, published at 11:00, 11:30 and 12:00.

Alongside them: four topic pages, two series, and a bilingual menu.

Anyone looking at numbers like that thinks of a machine spraying out text.
That is a reasonable guess.
It just explains the wrong thing.

## The bottleneck was never the thinking

Some of the thoughts collected here have been following me around for decades.

Chaotic systems interested me more than anything else when I was young.
Conway's *Life* and the Mandelbrot set were the first two programs I wrote on my first own PC in the late eighties, in Turbo Pascal, saved onto 5¼-inch floppies.
A few decades have passed since, and nothing about that has changed except the hardware.

None of it was written down.
Those thoughts lived in the damp darkness of my head, with no structure, no order, and not a single finished sentence.
There was no shortage of material and no shortage of interest.
What was missing was the part of the work that sits between having an idea and having a readable text: finding an outline, fixing an order, picking examples, doing all of it a second time in another language, building diagrams, checking links, publishing.

For me, that part has always cost a multiple of what the thinking costs.
It is the reason most people with interesting thoughts never write them down.

For the past week, that part has cost about as much as talking about it.

So what is happening here is not more thinking.
It is drainage.
Thirty years of backlog running out at whatever rate the pipe allows — which is why the first week looks like a conveyor belt when it is really an emptying.

## What the tool does not supply

The idea that a language model could fill a blog like this on its own runs into a plain question: how would it know what is going on here?

Three of the posts so far form the series [Reverberations](/series/reverberations/): three debugging sessions on a Chuwi laptop, each of them a good theory broken by a measurement.
Silent speakers, a volume control nobody has touched in years, a boot that got two and a half seconds shorter.
Every single number in them comes from that one machine.
No model on earth knows those numbers before somebody measures them.

The same goes for the knob with a display sitting on my desk.
There are two microcontrollers on that board, and which of them shows up on the USB-C port depends on which way round the plug is inserted — that much the vendor does document.
It also states that the board has two rotary encoders.
The pins say otherwise: not two channels a quarter cycle apart, but one pulse line per direction of turn — read them as an encoder and you get a counter that steps one out and one back forever.
No datasheet says so, because at this point the datasheet claims the opposite.
My notes say so, because I traced 134 steps to find out what those two lines actually do.

The same goes for the decision that half-finished notes about this blog may reach my phone but nobody else's, and for every number above.
The material comes from here.
The tool takes over the road from material to text — and that road is where everything got stuck for thirty years.

## Why the number means something anyway

A high cadence is not a mark of quality.
It is a symptom, and it matters what of.

If it comes from pointing a model at a subject area and publishing whatever falls out, it says nothing about the author and little that is good about the texts.

If it comes from an old backlog draining, then it is finite.
At some point the backlog is worked off, and the pace drops back to whatever new thinking supplies.
That is exactly what I expect.
The first week of a blog with thirty years of run-up is not its normal speed.

## What transfers

If you are carrying around a stack of unwritten thoughts yourself, one distinction is worth making: are you missing the material, or the road from material to text?

If the material is missing, no tool will help.
A model cannot know what has occupied you for twenty years, and it cannot invent it either.

If the road is missing, its price has dropped by an order of magnitude over the past two years, and you probably will not notice until you try it once.
Do not pick the thought that matters most to you.
Pick the one you have carried longest.
It is the most chewed over, and it puts up the least resistance to being given a form.
