## The display case

On the first Sunday in September I was strolling through the fair of the [Antikmeile](https://antikmeile-suarezstrasse.de/), the antiques mile on Suarezstraße in Berlin.
A dealer had laid out a scant dozen old English paperweights, carefully protected in a sturdy display case.

Each of them is really just a lump of clear glass that sits comfortably in the hand.
Inside, though, lies a rich life of coloured glass, and you can sense how much work it took to make.
Each one is unique.
In one, a small frog sat on a leaf.
The price tag on it said several hundred euros.

Several hundred euros for a chunk of glass that is meant to keep letters from blowing away.
A grey stone from the roadside would do the same, wouldn't it?

A child stood in front of the case, amazed.
"Mum, look, aren't they beautiful!"
And right after: "What are they good for? What can you do with them?"

In two sentences the child asked two questions that we like to fold into one.
The first asks about value.
The second asks about function and use.
With this object the answers lie as far apart as they can.

## Created

The English paperweights of the 19th century were made in small numbers.
George Bacchus & Sons are said to have [made them for only about six years, some 500 pieces in total](https://scottishantiques.com/paperweights/Old-English-Paperweights/).

A piece like that is created with open eyes, in the very moment it comes into being.
The glassblower sets the coloured canes, cases them in clear glass, shapes the dome while it is hot.
What comes out cannot be repeated, not even by him.

It was meant for the hand that puts it on the desk and for the eye that falls on it.
Its practical use was small from the start.
The glassblower wanted to make something beautiful, and he did.
Today these pieces mostly stand in display cases, where no hand reaches them and no letter lies beneath them.

## Given form

I have described the Curta here before, in [The silky sound of mechanical arithmetic](/posts/the-silky-sound-of-arithmetic/).
A mechanical calculator, small enough for one hand.
Curt Herzstark finished designing it while he was a prisoner in the Buchenwald concentration camp.
That is the opposite of the picture we have of engineering: no engineer in a white coat at the drawing board with ruler and slide rule, no workshop, no prototype.
Most of it must have taken shape in his head.

The Curta is a deterministic algorithm you can hold in your hand.
You set a number, turn the crank, and the calculation runs through the gears.
Each one carries a serial number on its base, which makes it one of a kind.
In its function, each one has been reliably the same for decades.

It, too, was meant for hand and eye, and it, too, now stands in museum display cases.
With the Curta that is for another reason: its beauty is the beauty of its function.
It is beautiful because it does so exactly what it was built for.

The paperweight has little use and high value.
The Curta has high use and high value.
One is created, the other given form.
Material, form and procedure lie in one hand for both: in the glass and the glassblower's hand, in the brass and the designer's head.

## A find without a function

On 23 September Anthropic reported that [Claude agents had discovered a new enzyme system](https://www.anthropic.com/news/claude-discovers-novel-enzyme-system).
A research group the company formed in spring 2026 had roughly 950 agents search a large database of DNA sequences for 21 hours.
The agents read literature, analysed data and formed hypotheses.
By Anthropic's own account, the humans were involved only in the initial prompt and in the lab work.

What the agents found is a previously uncharacterised system in bacteriophages, viruses that infect bacteria.
Anthropic calls it *array-associated reverse transcriptases*, ART for short, and describes a resemblance to CRISPR.
Scientists then expressed the protein in the lab and characterised it biochemically and structurally.
So the system exists.
What it is good for, nobody knows yet: the work on its function, Anthropic writes, is ongoing.

That is exactly the child's question, asked of a completely different object.
With the paperweight the function was clear and small, and everyone agreed on the value.
With the Curta both were high and both were clear.
With this find, value, function and use are all three open.
And no hand or eye found it, but a crowd of agents reading a database.

## Hand and eye

Curta and paperweight both fit in a hand because humans have hands.
Their size, their weight and their beauty are measured against hand and eye.

An AI has neither.
It finds something whose value, function and use are open, and puts it in front of people who test it.
That leaves questions I have no answer to:

- What, for an AI, is the equivalent of hand and eye?
- What could an AI create, or want to create, because it is of high value? And to whom?
- What can an AI give form to because it is of great use to people?
- Can an AI also create for the sake of beauty alone?

And if it can: is that AI then an AGI?
