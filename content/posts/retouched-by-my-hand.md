The first part of this series ends on a question: what the division of labour is called when the material very much does come from me.
Here that is the normal case.
I bring the question, the experience, the number and the judgement.
Claude brings the sentences, plus everything that surrounds a post: creating and moving notes, running the checkers, bringing the other language version into line, publishing.

There is a word for this situation that is older than any debate about AI: **workshop**.

## Summary

- Whoever bought a picture from Rubens could end up with a workshop piece.
  The master designed, assistants executed, he retouched, and it was sold under his name.
- In 1618 Rubens sent a buyer a price list that states, for every picture, how much of it is his hand.
  His own work cost more.
- Auction houses still use fixed grades for this: the name alone, "Studio of", "Circle of", "Manner of".
- Which hand painted which passage often cannot be settled from the picture itself.
  The Rembrandt Research Project kept a category of its own for that.
- The comparison carries a long way but fails in three places: self-reference, memory and cost.
- The workshop was acceptable wherever the buyer learned whose hand painted what.
  Here that statement appears once for the whole blog, not picture by picture.

## A price list from 1618

In the spring of 1618 Peter Paul Rubens was negotiating with the English envoy Sir Dudley Carleton.
Carleton owned a collection of antique sculpture, Rubens wanted it and offered paintings in exchange.
With his letter of 28 April he enclosed a list of the pictures in his house.
For each it gives a price and who painted it.

A Prometheus bound, 500 florins: "Original, by my hand, and the Eagle done by Snyders."
A Daniel amidst many lions, 600: "Original, the whole by my hand."
An Achilles clothed as a woman, also 600: "done by the best of my scholars, and the whole retouched by my hand."
A Susanna, 300: "done by one of my scholars, the whole, however, retouched by my hand."

Frans Snyders was an animal painter, a specialist Rubens brought in for the eagle.
Carleton took only the originals.
Rubens replied on 12 May that he was perfectly content with that.
The others, he added, were not mere copies but "so well retouched by my hand that with difficulty they would be distinguished from originals, notwithstanding which they are put down at a much lower price".

The quotations follow the English translation [W. Noel Sainsbury published in 1859](https://archive.org/details/originalunpublis00sain).
The letters themselves are in Italian.

That is the workshop in two letters.
The master fixes the commission and the composition, paints the passages that matter and hands the rest to assistants.
In 2024 an exhibition at the Prado broke Rubens's workshop down into its stages, [as a review of the catalogue describes](https://hnanews.org/hnar/reviews/rubenss-workshop/): priming, drawing, dead colour, applying colour, retouching, varnishing.
Only some of that is painting in the narrow sense.

## Why the word fits

**The workshop does more than paint.**
A post here is the smaller part of the work.
Around it sit an idea note with a status, checkers for style, numbers and links, the second language version, publishing, and a reading vault for the phone.
A word that means only the writing leaves half of that out.

**The workshop is openly declared.**
The art trade has a fixed vocabulary for the question of whose hand painted a picture.
Christie's explains it in its [conditions of sale](http://files.artsy.net/documents/christies-conditions-of-sale-prints-multiples-september-2018.pdf).
"Studio of" or "Workshop of" means "a work executed in the studio or workshop of the artist, possibly under his supervision".
"Circle of" means "a work of the period of the artist and showing his influence".
"Manner of" means "a work executed in the artist's style but of a later date".
The house guarantees authorship only for the name without a qualifier.
For every other grade it assumes no risk.

That is exactly what the [notice on the entry pages](/posts/) of this blog does.
It names Claude Code before anyone has to ask.

**The decisions stay at the top.**
The assistants deliver passages, not decisions.
Which picture Rubens retouches himself and which he offers as an original is his call.
Here I decide what gets measured, what counts as evidence and when a post goes online.
A draft I do not want does not appear.

## Which hand painted which passage

The workshop also knows the dispute that follows from this division of labour.

In 1968 Bob Haak and Josua Bruyn started the Rembrandt Research Project in Amsterdam.
According to the Dutch museum network [CODART](https://www.codart.nl/research-study/a-corpus-of-rembrandt-paintings-volume-vi-rembrandt-s-paintings-revisited-a-complete-survey-presented/), its aim was to rid Rembrandt's oeuvre of paintings that harmed the image of the painter.
The [first volume](https://rembrandtdatabase.org/literature/corpus.html), published in 1982, sorts into three categories.
A is paintings by Rembrandt, C is paintings that are not by him.
Between them lies B: paintings whose authorship by Rembrandt "cannot be positively either accepted or rejected".

The preface explains why that middle is needed.
Materials and methods barely differ between Rembrandt and his circle, because both follow general 17th-century workshop practice.
Even when tree rings show that two panels came from the same tree, one is not prevented from giving one to Rembrandt and the other to "somebody from his workshop".
And even a thorough scientific examination of every picture would, in the end, leave the decision to "considerations of a very different kind".

The verdicts did not stay put.
When the project ended in 2014 with its sixth volume, Ernst van de Wetering counted 340 autograph paintings, against 280 in 1986.
Some sixty pictures that had been rejected before were taken back in.

The best-known case happened outside the project.
The ["Man with the Golden Helmet"](https://smb.museum-digital.de/object/60997) came to Berlin as a Rembrandt in 1897.
In 1986, after technical and art-historical examination, the Gemäldegalerie withdrew the attribution for good, and it has since counted as a work from Rembrandt's circle.
The association that owns the picture puts it in one sentence: ["Not a Rembrandt, but not a forgery either."](https://kaiser-friedrich-museumsverein.de/der-mann-mit-dem-goldhelm/)

The same question arises for every post here, and it is no easier to settle from the text than from the painting.
That is the subject of the next part.

## Where the comparison falls short

**Self-reference.**
Rubens's assistants painted saints, hunts and gods.
Here the assistant writes about what it is like to work with it.
It is tool and subject at once, in this paragraph too.
A person in that role would at least be an interested party with a say in it.
The catalogue has no grade for that case.

**Memory.**
A journeyman who has spent years in a workshop comes to know, without being told, how the master paints hands.
Claude starts every session with no memory of the last one.
What is meant to last is kept in files: in working instructions and memory notes that I read and correct.
They are prostheses for the part of working together that people get for free.

**Cost.**
A picture from the workshop tied up weeks of work, materials and wages.
A draft here costs minutes.
It is not free, as [The most expensive answer is yes](/posts/the-most-expensive-answer-is-yes/) works out.
But a draft that cost minutes is thrown away more easily than one that cost weeks.
That changes the way of working, not only the price.

## Under which conditions it was acceptable

The real work the comparison does is a shift.
It turns the question "Is this still yours?" into another one: under which conditions was this always acceptable?

The answer is not "always".
Michiel Franken and Jaap van der Veen have [looked at](https://www.theleidencollection.com/essays/the-signing-of-paintings-by-rembrandt-and-his-contemporaries/) what a signature meant on Dutch paintings of the 17th century.
When a painter signed a picture that a pupil had painted and he had gone over, that did not mean he considered it autograph.
The signature "authenticated such paintings as studio pieces".
Adriaen van der Werff noted, for a picture of 1718, nine weeks of his own work and thirteen weeks by his brother Pieter.
It is signed with his name alone.

Buyers read the same mark differently.
"The signature seems to have had a different meaning for makers than for buyers," the two authors write.
In 1684 the Antwerp flower painter Nicolaes van Verendael was asked to sign two pictures that a former pupil had painted and he had painted over.
He refused at first and gave in only when the frame maker promised not to sell them in Antwerp.
Later he declared before the guild and a notary that he did not regard them as his work.

So the workshop was acceptable wherever the buyer learned what he was getting: in Rubens's price list, in the grades of the catalogue.
It became delicate where the name stood alone and everyone read something different into it.

The notice on the entry pages of this blog says: "every post on this blog is written using Claude Code".
And: "I read, check and edit everything before it goes up."
In the language of the catalogue that is "Workshop of"; in Rubens's, "retouched by my hand".
It appears once, for everything.
A list like the one from 1618, post by post, does not exist here.
The [first part](/posts/who-built-the-chain/) showed with one series that a single statement for everything can be too little.

## What carries over

Whoever works with a workshop does not owe autograph work.
They owe a statement of how much of it is autograph, placed where the reader will find it.
